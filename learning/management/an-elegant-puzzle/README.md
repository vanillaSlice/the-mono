# An Elegant Puzzle: Systems of Engineering Management by Will Larson

## Organisation

* Managers should support six to eight engineers.
* Managers of managers should support four to six managers.
* On-call rotations want eight engineers.
* Teams of fewer than four people aren't teams.
* Four stages of a team:
  * Falling Behind: add new people to the team (net new people, not existing people in the organisation).
  * Treading Water: reduce work in progress and get things completed.
  * Repaying Debt: add time by pushing back, allow debt to be repaid before innovating.
  * Innovating: add slack, allow that innovation to continue.
* When things get hard, stay the path. That's where you learn.
* Focus on one team at a time (e.g. rapid hiring).
* Don't disassemble high performing teams.
* Adding more engineers adds more problems: interviews, training, more deployments, more incidents, and can slow things down.
* Pick your battles. You can't do everything to a high standard. There'll be certain things you're doing poorly.
* Have a succession plan. Work on delegating and writing up what it is you do.

## Tools

* Try thinking in systems, it's surprising where they can be applied.
* Engineering and product leadership should be separate but sometimes you need to fill in that role. A system to help with that:
  * Problem Discovery
  * Problem Selection
  * Solution Validation
  * Execution
  * Repeat
* Try using Vision and Strategy documents.
* Strategy documents are for solving particular problems, there'll be a lot of these.
* Vision documents are for the future, there should be fewer of these.
* Use metrics to guide change.
* Migrations are the only way to tackle large technical debt. Ensure you:
  * Derisk by creating and iterating on a design document. Working with the most difficult teams to avoid quick wins.
  * Enable by providing documentation and as much self-serve.
  * Finish. Get to that 100% even if it means doing things by yourself. Only celebrate once at 100%.
* Reorganisations:
  * Validate if it is necessary.
  * Choose groups before individuals.
  * Work out team size.
  * Staff the teams (preferably with existing staff).
  * Execute the changes.
* Try organising learning communities.
* Master time management (blocks in calendar, evaluate how you spend your time each quarter).
* Try organising working groups to apply consistency across the organisation.

## Approaches

* Have policies in place and limit exceptions. For example, if on-site members of staff need to belong to a particular team.
* Treat others as you would want to be treated.
* With great relationships in place, you can pretty much solve anything.
* With the right people, any process works, and with the wrong people, no process works.
* Solve or approach the hard problems now, don't postpone.
* Treat management/leadership positions as new roles, what worked in one context might not work in another.
* When a problem comes your way:
  * Close Out: make a decision such that it never comes back to you.
  * Solve: solve it such that you wouldn't need to spend the same amount of time on it in six months.
  * Delegate: hand over to someone responsible for it.

## Culture

* Inclusivity involves encouraging opportunities and membership.
* Opportunity can be ensuring there are plenty of project leadership opportunities, not going to the same people.
* Membership means feeling part of the team. This can be team lunches or socials.
* Open up project leadership for application. Select people who could use it as a learning opportunity.
* Your peers are you first team, the team you support is your next team.
* Kill hero programmers, let them burnout or kill the environment that breeds them.
* Learn from mistakes and move on.

## Careers

* Career ladders will adapt over time.
* Iterate on your hiring process, get feedback from candidates, find out how other companies do it.
* Create new roles if needed and invest in them.
